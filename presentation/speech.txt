PART 1. PROBLEM  (planned 2 min, script 2:00, 0:00 to 2:00)
============================================================================================

[Slide 1] Title slide
(0:00 to 0:30)
Hello everyone, we are group 7: Arseniy, Polina, Elizaveta and Vladislav. We are presenting
"On the Rise of FinTechs: Credit Scoring Using Digital Footprints" by Tobias Berg, Valentin
Burg, Ana Gombović and Manju Puri, published in the Review of Financial Studies in 2020. The
question is simple: can the traces we leave on a website tell a lender as much as a credit
bureau?

[Slide 2] At a German online shop, simple traces like the device spread default rates as widely as the credit bureau score
(0:30 to 2:00)
Imagine you buy a sofa online and pay by invoice two weeks later. For the shop, every such
order is a small loan. Before it ships, it knows your name, your address and your email,
and, for free, a few things the website records anyway: whether you use a phone or a
computer, Android or iPhone, how you came to the site, and the time of the order. This chart
is drawn from the paper's Table 3. Each dot is a group of customers and its default rate.
The orange row is the credit bureau score in five quintiles: from 2.1 percent in the worst
fifth to 0.4 percent in the best. Now the navy rows. Orders from an Android phone default at
2.5 percent, from a Mac at 0.7. Yahoo users at 2 percent, T-Online users at 0.5. A typing
error in the email address: 5 percent. These simple traces spread customers as widely as the
bureau does. If that holds in general, a fintech can lend without the information advantage
of a bank, and some of the two billion adults without access to banks could get credit.

PART 2. METHOD  (planned 3 min, script 2:55, 2:00 to 4:55)
============================================================================================

[Slide 3] Every purchase on invoice is a short loan, and the website records ten footprint variables for free
(2:00 to 3:25)
The data comes from a German online furniture shop, similar to Wayfair: 270,399 purchases on
invoice between October 2015 and December 2016. Default means that the invoice was still
unpaid after three reminders and went to a debt collection agency, on average three and a
half months after the order. That happens to 0.94 percent of the customers with a bureau
score. For every order above 100 euros the shop requests a bureau score, and 6 percent of
the customers have none. They default at 2.5 percent. Next to the score, the shop records
ten footprint variables, and the authors group them by what they stand for. Income and
wealth: the device, the operating system and the email host. T-Online is a paid service with
richer customers, Yahoo and Hotmail are free and old. Character: the channel, for example a
paid ad or a price comparison site, and the time of the order. And reputation and care: a
name or a number in the email address, typing only in lower case, and a typing error in the
email.

[Slide 4] Three logit models are compared by their AUC: how often a random defaulter looks riskier than a random payer
(3:25 to 4:55)
How do they compare the footprint with the bureau score? They fit three logit models of
default: on the bureau score alone, on the footprint alone, and on both. Each model gives
every customer a predicted probability of default, and each is judged by its AUC, the area
under the ROC curve. In words: take one customer who defaulted and one who paid, at random.
The AUC is the chance that the model calls the defaulter riskier. Fifty percent is a coin
flip. In credit scoring, 60 percent is good when information is scarce and 70 is the goal
when it is rich. The difference between two AUCs is tested with the test of DeLong, and
overfitting with cross-validation. The shop's data is private, so we take two routes, shown
at the bottom. First, we rebuild a sample from the paper's own tables and check that our
code gives back its numbers. Second, we run the same tests on public data: 307,511 loans of
the lender Home Credit, which has an external score and an application form that leaves a
similar footprint.

============================================================================================
PART 3. RESULTS AND OUR REPRODUCTION  (planned 5 min, script 4:55, 4:55 to 9:50)
============================================================================================

[Slide 5] On a sample rebuilt from the paper's tables, our code redraws Figure 4, but with a weaker footprint
(4:55 to 6:05)
First the check. From the shares in Table 3 and the coefficients in Table 5 we drew 254,808
customers with a score and 15,591 without, and ran our code on them. On the left is Figure 4
of the paper, on the right the same three ROC curves from our replica, on the same axes. The
bureau score comes back at 68.5 percent against 68.3, both together at 73.3 against 73.6,
and the footprint of customers without a score at 72.7 against 72.2. So our code and the
paper's tables agree. Only the footprint alone is weaker, 65 against 69.6. We draw every
trait on its own, with the bureau score held fixed, while in the real data risky traits come
together and share some information with the score. And the paper reports only 6 of its 18
email hosts; with those 6 its own footprint has 68.7 percent.

[Slide 6] Night applications default most and evening ones least, at the German shop and at Home Credit
(6:05 to 7:10)
Now Home Credit. Its 307,511 loan applications are public on Kaggle, with a default rate of
8 percent. As the bureau score we use the external score called EXT_SOURCE_3. As the
footprint we take nine things the application records that say nothing about income or debt:
the hour and the weekday, whether an email, a home phone and a work phone were given, when
the phone number last changed, who came along, a contact address in another city, and
whether the housing questions were left blank. The first result: the hour works in the same
direction as in the paper. Night is the riskiest time and evening the safest, in both
datasets. But at the shop night buyers default at twice the average, at Home Credit only 17
percent above it.

[Slide 7] On Home Credit the footprint alone ranks borrowers far worse than the bureau score, but still adds to it
(7:10 to 8:10)
This is the main result. On the left, Figure 4 of the paper: the footprint at 69.6 percent
beats the bureau score at 68.3, and both together reach 73.6. On the right, our ROC curves
on the same axes. The external score has almost the same AUC as the German bureau score,
67.9 percent. But our footprint alone reaches only 58.3, almost ten points below the score.
So the paper's first claim, that the footprint is as good as a bureau score, does not carry
over. The second claim does: adding the footprint to the score raises the AUC to 69.1, and
the DeLong test says this gain is far from chance. It is only 1.2 points, though, against
5.3 in the paper.

[Slide 8] As in the paper, the footprint and the bureau score are almost unrelated, so they complement each other
(8:10 to 9:05)
Why does a weak footprint still add something? Because it knows different things. The paper
shows this with Figure 5. Every dot is a customer, placed by its percentile under the bureau
score and under the footprint. The cloud has no shape, and the R squared is 1 percent. Our
version on Home Credit looks the same: an R squared of 0.8 percent, a correlation of 9
percent against about 10 in the paper, and a Cramér's V between the score and each footprint
variable of at most 0.08, against 0.07. On this point the two datasets agree almost exactly.
Most clients with a good score have an average footprint, and the other way round.

[Slide 9] Without a bureau score the footprint works at least as well, in the paper and in our data
(9:05 to 9:50)
The paper's most hopeful result is about people without a credit history. For the 6 percent
of customers without a bureau score, the footprint works at least as well: 72.2 percent
against 69.6 for the others, and almost the same out of sample. We see the same pattern at
Home Credit. Clients without an external score default more often, 9.3 percent against 7.8,
and the footprint ranks them slightly better than the others: 59.7 against 58.3 percent. The
level is far below the paper's, but the direction is the same: the footprint does not need a
credit file to work.

============================================================================================
PART 4. CRITIQUE  (planned 3 min, script 3:10, 9:50 to 13:00)
============================================================================================

[Slide 10] Out of sample the AUCs barely fall; the paper's 3.9-point drop is mostly small-sample noise
(9:50 to 11:00)
Could these results be overfit? An AUC measured on the customers the model was fitted on is
too optimistic, the more so with few defaults per coefficient. The paper's customers without
a score have only about 390 defaults. The authors answer with cross-validation: fit on one
random half, predict the other, a hundred times. With a score the AUCs fall by less than a
point, unless many fixed effects are added, and without a score by 3.9 points. We ran the
same test on our replica, where by construction there is nothing to find beyond the paper's
coefficients. It loses 3 points without a score. So most of the paper's drop is what a small
sample does by chance, not a sign of fitting noise. On Home Credit, with thousands of
defaults, the drops are a fraction of a point.

[Slide 11] On a new dataset four of the paper's five claims hold, but the footprint alone falls far short of the bureau score
(11:00 to 11:50)
Our upgrade is the new dataset itself, so here are the paper's five claims next to it. The
footprint and the score are almost unrelated: holds. Together they beat either one: holds,
but with a gain of 1.2 points instead of 5.3. The footprint works without a score: holds.
The AUCs survive out of sample: holds. But the boldest claim, a footprint as good as a
bureau score, does not: 58 against 68 percent. This does not depend on which external score
we call the bureau score: with the other two the gap is 7 to 9 points. And a wider
footprint, with fields about the job added, still reaches only 61 percent.

[Slide 12] The open question is whether the footprint can replace a credit bureau, or only add to it
(11:50 to 13:00)
First the assumptions. The shop's customers stand in for borrowers in general, but these are
orders above 100 euros, loans of about three and a half months, and the shop had already
turned away customers with a predicted default above 10 percent, using the same score and
footprint. On data snooping: the categories were chosen on the same data. With only the 6
email hosts they report, the footprint has 68.7 percent, barely above the bureau score at
68.3, and even with 18 hosts the lead is significant only at the 10 percent level. A missing
baseline: the footprint is never compared with a model built on the cheap data every lender
already collects, like income or job. The authors do check fixed effects, a stricter default
definition and sample splits. What would break the story is a different setting, and Home
Credit is one.

PART 5. NEXT STEPS  (planned 2 min, script 2:00, 13:00 to 15:00)
============================================================================================

[Slide 13] With three more months we would test a richer footprint, its fairness and its stability
(13:00 to 15:00)
With three more months, this is what we would do. First, a footprint closer to the paper's.
Lending Club published about two million loans with the job title as the borrower typed it,
so we could test the paper's lower case effect in American data. Second, fairness. The paper
expects regulators to ask whether the footprint is a proxy for characteristics like gender
or age, which American credit bureaus may not use. We would measure how well the footprint
predicts them. Third, stability. The Estonian lender Bondora records the hour each
application was signed, from 2009 to 2021, so we could see whether the night effect fades
over the years. And fourth, a flexible model like gradient boosting instead of a logit, to
see whether the same footprint holds more information. To conclude. With our own code, the
paper's tables give back its AUCs, so the paper is consistent with itself. On public loan
data, the footprint and the bureau score are nearly unrelated, they add up, and the
footprint works without a credit file. But here the footprint alone is far weaker than a
bureau score. Part of the reason may be our variables: Home Credit records no device and no
email host, and those were the strongest traces at the German shop. So the paper's idea
survives, but its boldest claim needs a richer footprint to be tested. Simple traces help a
lender; on this evidence they do not replace a credit bureau.

QUESTIONS WE SHOULD BE READY FOR (10 min of questions)
============================================================================================

1. Why did you not use the paper's data?
   It belongs to a German online shop and is not public. We did two things instead: a
replica drawn from the paper's own tables, to check our code against its numbers, and the
same tests on the public Home Credit loans.

2. What exactly is EXT_SOURCE_3?
   Home Credit only calls it a normalized score from an external data source. It behaves
like a bureau score: alone it has an AUC of 67.9%, almost the paper's 68.3%. With the other
two external scores our conclusions do not change: the footprint is 7.0 to 8.6 points below
them.

3. Is your footprint not simply a bad copy of the paper's?
   Partly, and that is the main limit of our test. Home Credit has no device, operating
system or email host, and it records who came along to apply, so many applications seem to
be made in person. We took what the application records about how it was made. Adding fields
about the job raises the footprint only to 60.8%. So what fails to carry over may be these
variables, not the idea itself.

4. Why is the footprint weaker in your replica than in the paper?
   65.0% against 69.6%. We draw each variable on its own and use the coefficients measured
with the bureau score held fixed, while in the real data risky traits come together and
overlap with the score. The paper also reports only 6 of its 18 email hosts; with 6 hosts
its own AUC is 68.7%. The model with both, which does not suffer from the overlap, comes
back at 73.3% against 73.6%.

5. What does a difference of one or two points of AUC mean in practice?
   The AUC is a ranking measure, so a higher AUC usually means fewer defaulters among the
approved customers at the same approval rate. In the paper the lead of the footprint over
the score is 1.3 points and significant only at the 10% level; the gain from combining them,
5.3 points, is the robust result.

6. What is the DeLong test?
   A test for the difference between two AUCs measured on the same customers. It uses each
customer's contribution to both AUCs, so it accounts for the two models being correlated. On
Home Credit the gain of the combined model over the score has a z of 16.6.

7. Why N×2-fold cross-validation and not one test sample?
   Every customer is predicted once out of sample, so the AUC uses the whole sample, and
repeating the split averages out the luck of one split. We used the paper's 100 repetitions.

8. Why is the out-of-sample drop so small in Home Credit?
   It has 19,148 defaults with a score and 5,677 without, for about 20 coefficients. The
paper's customers without a score have 388 defaults, and our replica of that size loses 3.0
points by chance alone.

9. Who are the clients without a score in Home Credit?
   Clients for whom EXT_SOURCE_3 is missing, one in five. Home Credit does not say why. Like
the paper's unscorable customers they default more often: 9.3% against 7.8%.

10. Could customers game the footprint?
   Some variables are costly to change, like buying an iPhone or a paid email account;
others are habits, like shopping at night. The authors discuss this as the Lucas critique:
once lenders use the footprint, people may change it, and its power could fall or even rise.
Nobody has tested it yet.

11. Is using the footprint fair?
   It may be a proxy for characteristics lenders may not use. The paper raises this for
regulators but does not measure it. It is our second next step.

12. Does the shop's own screening bias the results?
   Possibly. The shop turned away customers with a predicted default above 10%, using the
bureau score and the footprint. This removes the worst customers on both measures, so both
AUCs are probably lower than in an unscreened population, and we cannot say which one loses
more.

13. How is the time of day measured at Home Credit?
   HOUR_APPR_PROCESS_START, the hour at which the client started the application. We cut it
into the paper's four bands: night 0–6, morning 6–12, afternoon 12–18 and evening 18–24.
